Abstract

When a system has to decide whether something is suspicious enough to act on, where exactly should the line be drawn? This project argues that the line is not a fact waiting to be discovered, but a value judgement about which mistake matters more, and that it should move whenever that judgement changes rather than being xed once and left alone. The idea is framed around an old philosophical puzzle, the Sorites Paradox: a heap of sand does not stop being a heap after just one grain is removed, and yet removing enough grains clearly does leave a non-heap, so no single grain ever marks the true boundary. Deciding whether a transaction is fraudulent enough to block runs into a quiet version of exactly the same problem.

The practical outcome is Threshold Lab, an interactive tool built around a bank fraud scenario. Rather than using a xed, one-size- ts-all cut-o , it asks what the two kinds of mistake actually cost wrongly blocking a genuine customer, versus missing a real fraud and works out, from those costs, exactly where the line should sit. It compares a few di erent ways of building and checking the underlying prediction model, and lets a user change their assumptions about cost on the y and immediately see how the decision, and its nancial consequences, change.

This project’s original idea is the Contested Zone: rather than only agging cases the model itself is unsure about, it identi es the transactions whose outcome would ip depending on which of two equally reasonable views about cost is taken. A modest but meaningful share of transactions, representing a real amount of money, turn out to fall into this zone of genuine disagreement between reasonable people, rather than into any error the model is making. The dissertation also reports, honestly rather than selectively, a result that did not go as expected: the simplest model tried performed better than the more sophisticated ones on this particular dataset, and the write-up discusses why. It closes with a candid evaluation of the nished system against its original goals, what a proper study involving real people would still need to add, and how several classical philosophical responses to the Sorites Paradox each point towards a di erent way such a system could be built.

Chapter 1

Introduction and Background

1.1            Background and Motivation

Automated fraud detection is, at its core, a continuous risk-estimation problem wearing a binary decision’s clothes. A modern classi er does not actually decide whether a transaction is fraudulent; it estimates a probability, P(fraud | x), that lies somewhere on the continuum between 0 and 1. Somewhere along that continuum, a decision threshold τ must be chosen, and every transaction scoring at or above τ is blocked, agged or investigated, while everything below it proceeds untouched. In almost every treatment of classi cation encountered during this M.Sc. programme, and in the overwhelming majority of publicly available tutorials and libraries, that threshold is τ = 0.5 a value chosen not because it minimises any real cost, but because it is the arithmetic midpoint of the unit interval.

This project starts from the observation that τ = 0.5 is very rarely the right answer for a real deployment, because the two mistakes a fraud classi er can make are not equally costly. A false positive (blocking a genuine transaction) costs a bank customer friction, and the bank a support-desk interaction and some reputational goodwill. A false negative (missing a genuine fraud) costs the bank the full value of the fraudulent transaction, and potentially more in downstream liability. Elkan (2001) formalised this observation into a single, elegant result the cost-sensitive threshold theorem which is the mathematical spine of this project and is introduced properly in Section 2.4.

What makes the threshold question philosophically interesting, rather than merely an engineering parameter to tune, is that it reproduces almost exactly the structure of the Sorites Paradox, one of the oldest puzzles in philosophy. Attributed to Eubulides of Miletus and still actively discussed today (Hyde and Ra man, 2018; Keefe, 2000; Oms and Zardini, 2019), the paradox observes that a heap of sand remains a heap after any single grain is removed no individual grain’s removal is ever the decisive one and yet if the process is repeated enough times, the heap unarguably becomes a non-heap. Wright (1975) formalised the intuition that drives the paradox as the Tolerance Principle: predicates like is a heap should be tolerant of su ciently small changes in the underlying quantity, and yet any predicate that is tolerant along the whole sequence, from 10,000 grains down to zero, leads to a contradiction. Figure 1.1 dramatises three snapshots along exactly this sequence, and the interactive version built for this project (Section 2.6) lets a user walk through it themselves.

A fraud classi er faces a quiet, numerical version of the same problem. Two transactions that the model scores at 0.5002 and 0.4998 respectively are, for any practical purpose, indistinguishable the 0.0004 gap is comfortably inside ordinary model noise. Yet a hard threshold at τ = 0.5 assigns them opposite labels: one customer is blocked, the other is not (this exact worked example, with |0.5002 − 0.4998| = 0.0004 exceeding a stated tolerance ε = 0.0002, is reproduced live in the dashboard’s Home page and quoted with its true computed values in Section 3.2). Treating this as a bug to be engineered away misses the point: some amount of boundary-drawing is unavoidable once a continuous score must produce a discrete action. The more useful question, and the one this project tries to operationalise, is not where is the true boundary? but how far does the boundary move as reasonable cost assumptions change, and how many transactions like this pair fall into the disputed territory in between?

1.2          Problem Statement

Input: a labelled or unlabelled stream of credit card transactions, each described by a transaction time, an amount, and a set of anonymised numerical features, together with a stated (or comparable) pair of business costs: the cost of a false positive, C(FP), and the cost of a false negative, C(FN).

Task: train a classi er to estimate P(fraud | x) for each transaction; convert that continuous estimate into a binary decision using a threshold that is derived from, and can be justi ed in terms of, the stated cost assumptions rather than xed by convention; and make the consequences of that derivation the resulting threshold, the confusion matrix it produces, the total expected cost, and the set of transactions whose label would change under a di erent but equally defensible cost assumption visible and explorable rather than buried inside a single accuracy number.

1.3           Aims and Requirements

The project’s aims, xed during the proposal stage and used throughout this dissertation as the yardstick against which the nished system is evaluated (see the self-evaluation in Section 3.6), were:

R1. Implement Elkan’s (2001) cost-sensitive threshold formula, τ∗ = C(FP)/(C(FP) + C(FN)), and expose it interactively so that changing the stated costs immediately recomputes τ∗, the confusion matrix and the total expected cost.

R2. Train and compare at least two classi ers of meaningfully di erent character on the same data: an interpretable linear baseline and a non-linear ensemble method.

R3. Compare at least two established strategies for handling severe class imbalance, since fraud datasets are characteristically imbalanced (the ULB dataset used here has a 0.172% fraud rate).

R4. Evaluate probability calibration explicitly, since Elkan’s formula assumes the underlying probabilities are trustworthy, using at least two post-hoc calibration techniques against an uncalibrated baseline, together with reliability diagrams and calibration-aware metrics rather than raw accuracy.

R5. Design and implement an original, well-de ned operationalisation of borderline or contested transactions that connects directly back to the Sorites framing, rather than treating vagueness as a purely philosophical aside.

R6. Package the whole system as an interactive, navigable dashboard usable by a nonprogrammer stakeholder, with exportable results (CSV) for further analysis outside the tool.

R7. Structure the codebase so that all non-presentation logic is independently unit-testable, and build a test suite that exercises the mathematical and statistical correctness of every core function.

R8. Use only ethically low-risk, already-anonymised data, and discuss the ethical dimensions of an automated system whose false positives and false negatives both a ect real people.

A dedicated Requirements Analysis chapter involving human participants was not undertaken for this project: no user interviews, surveys or requirements-elicitation workshops were run, since the requirements above were derived directly from the cost-sensitive learning and calibration literature and from the module’s own proposal-stage supervisory feedback, rather than from primary research with prospective users. This is revisited candidly as a limitation in Sections 3.6 and 5.2.

1.4             Brief Description of the Approach

The system, Threshold Lab, is a Streamlit dashboard backed by a small, dependency-light Python package (src/) with three modules data_loader.py, ml_engine.py and sorites_engine.py each free of any Streamlit import so that every function can be unit tested in isolation from the user interface. data_loader.py resolves the dataset (an uploaded or auto-detected CSV if supplied, otherwise a bundled synthetic generator reproducing the ULB dataset’s 0.172% fraud rate), performs a strati ed train/test split, and scales only the two features (Time and Amount) that the original dataset authors did not already transform via PCA. ml_engine.py’s

FraudMLPipeline class trains Logistic Regression, Random Forest (Breiman, 2001), or XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) (the last with a graceful fallback to Random Forest if the optional xgboost package is not installed), optionally applies SMOTE oversampling (Chawla et al., 2002) to the training fold, and always ts both a Platt-scaled (Platt, 1999) and an isotoniccalibrated variant alongside the raw model, computing calibration and ranking metrics for all three (Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, 2005; Davis and Goadrich, 2006). sorites_engine.py implements Elkan’s threshold formula, the cost curve, the twin-transaction worked example, and the project’s original contribution the Contested Zone. app.py contains no statistical logic of its own; it is a presentation-only layer that wires the three modules together behind a sidebar control panel (data source, classi er, imbalance strategy, calibration method) and ve content tabs (Sorites Paradox Primer, Data Explorer and Imbalance, Elkan’s Threshold Lab, Probability Calibration, and the Contested Zone), caching expensive computations with Streamlit’s

@st.cache_data and @st.cache_resource decorators. The full architecture is diagrammed in Figure 2.1 and discussed in depth in Chapter 2.

1.6              Terminology Used in This Dissertation

A small number of terms recur throughout this dissertation and are de ned here once rather than re-explained at each occurrence. Threshold (τ) is the cut-o probability above which a transaction is labelled fraud. τ∗ denotes speci cally the cost-optimal threshold produced by Elkan’s formula. Calibration refers to the correspondence between a model’s predicted probabilities and the true empirical frequency of the positive class at that predicted probability a well-calibrated model that predicts 0.8 should be right about 80% of the time among all transactions it scores near 0.8, which is a separate property from ranking transactions correctly by risk. The Contested Zone is this project’s own term (de ned precisely in Section 2.5) for the set of transactions whose label changes depending on which of two plausible cost-ratio assumptions is adopted. SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique, Chawla et al., 2002) is a technique for rebalancing a training set by synthesising new minority-class examples along the line segments joining existing minority-class neighbours, rather than by naively duplicating them.

2.1         Architecture

The single most important design decision in this project, made early and never reversed, was to keep every piece of statistical and mathematical logic completely free of the Streamlit framework. Figure 2.1 shows the resulting layering: a presentation layer (app.py, 1,515 lines) sits on top of a three-module, pure-Python package (src/), and nothing in that package imports Streamlit at all.

This decision was motivated by a problem observed while iterating on earlier prototypes of the dashboard during the proposal stage: with model training, cost-formula logic and UI code interleaved inside the same Streamlit callback functions, every change to the layout risked silently changing a number, and every change to a number required re-reading UI code to nd where it lived. Separating the two means the three src/ modules can be imported and unit tested with plain Python unittest, with no Streamlit test harness, browser automation or mocking required (Chapter 3), while app.py itself contains no statistical computation to get wrong beyond correctly wiring together calls into the tested modules. As Section 2.7 shows, that last clause turned out to matter more than expected: keeping computation out of app.py does not automatically keep data-wiring bugs out of it too, and the one substantive defect found during this project lived in exactly that presentation layer, in code that reconstructs which rows of the original dataframe correspond to the model’s test-set predictions.

Everything below the app.py box is pure pandas/NumPy/scikit-learn and is exercised directly by the automated test suite (Chapter 3); everything in the app.py box is presentation logic, which is precisely where the alignment defect described in Section 2.7 turned out to live.

The full 143MB le is not distributed with the assessment materials, so the archive instead bundles a much smaller, 5,000-row CSV carrying the identical schema, with 25 fraud rows (0.5%) oversampled relative to the dataset’s natural 0.172% rate so that a small sample still contains enough fraud examples to be useful for a quick demonstration. generate_synthetic_ulb_dataset() produces a dataset with the same schema and, by default, the same 0.172% fraud rate as the authentic ULB data, using independent Gaussian latent features for V1 V28 with ve of them (V4, V11, V12, V14, V17 chosen because the published exploratory literature on this dataset repeatedly identi es them as showing strong class separation) shifted for the fraud class, and a log-normal Amount distribution with a higher mean for fraud. This is a deliberate design trade-o , carried over unchanged from the proposal stage: a purely synthetic dataset cannot demonstrate that the pipeline generalises to real fraud patterns, but it guarantees the dashboard, and every number in this dissertation, can be reproduced deterministically by anyone who clones the repository with no dependency on a third-party download succeeding. All quantitative results reported in Chapter 3, unless stated otherwise, use this generator at its default 50,000-row size and random seed 42, since that is also the dashboard’s own default con guration.

2.2.2          Splitting and scaling

preprocess_data() performs a strati ed train/test split (80/20 by default) so that the alreadytiny fraud count is preserved proportionally in both folds an ordinary random split risks producing a test fold with almost no fraud examples at all, which would make every downstream metric meaningless. Per the design proposal, only Time and Amount are standard-scaled, t exclusively on the training fold and applied to the test fold to avoid data leakage; V1 V28 are left untouched because they are already the output of a PCA transformation performed by the dataset’s original authors, and re-scaling an already-whitened representation would not be well justi ed.

2.2.3               An invented but clearly-labelled feature glossary

One design choice deserves an explicit ethical note rather than only a technical one. data_loader.py exports PCA_FEATURE_DESCRIPTIONS, a dictionary that attaches a plausiblesounding, human-readable label to each anonymised component for example V14 is labelled

Transaction Velocity Anomaly (Primary Signal) and V17 Geolocation Distance Delta (Primary Signal) . It is important to state plainly, here and in Chapter 4, what these labels are not: the ULB dataset’s original authors have never published what V1 V28 actually represent, precisely because doing so could undermine the anonymisation the PCA transform is there to provide, so no such mapping is publicly known or knowable from the released data. This was a deliberate narrative-usability choice, made consciously rather than by oversight, but on re ection a more responsible implementation would either omit the labels entirely or caption them clearly, in the interface itself and not only in this dissertation, as illustrative ction rather than presenting them unquali ed alongside real statistical output. This is one of the concrete, speci c lessons carried into the self-re ection in Chapter 6.

2.3              Model and Calibration Design: ml_engine.py

2.3.1             The FraudMLPipeline class and model choice

ml_engine.py’s single public class, FraudMLPipeline, is constructed with a model type and an imbalance strategy, and exposes fit(), predict_probabilities() and evaluate_calibration(). Three classi ers are selectable in the sidebar, per requirement R2.: Logistic Regression, whose coe cients are directly interpretable and whose output is a smooth sigmoid of a linear combination of the input features; Random Forest (Breiman, 2001), a bagged ensemble of decision trees; and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016), a gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Only the rst two are guaranteed to run from a clean install of requirements.txt, which does not list xgboost as a dependency; when the package is not importable, FraudMLPipeline degrades gracefully and silently trains a Random Forest instead when ’xgboost’ is selected, so the dashboard never crashes, but a user on a minimal install who selects XGBoost Gradient Boosting is, without any on-screen indication, actually looking at a Random Forest’s output. This is a genuine gap between the UI’s advertised options and what requirements.txt guarantees, and it is listed as a concrete, low-e ort x in Section 5.2 (either add xgboost to requirements.txt, or surface the fallback to the user with a warning). For the same reason, and because it is the pairing requirements.txt actually supports out of the box, Logistic Regression and Random Forest are treated as this project’s primary two-model comparison satisfying requirement R2., with XGBoost reported throughout as a documented, working, but optional-dependency extra.

When class weighting is selected, scikit-learn’s class_weight=’balanced’ (Logistic Regression) or class_weight=’balanced_subsample’ (Random Forest) is used, and XGBoost’s scale_pos_weight is set from the training fold’s class ratio to the same e ect; when SMOTE is selected instead, no additional internal weighting is applied, since the training data has already been rebalanced before .fit() is called. A dedicated neural network was considered at proposal stage and rejected: it was judged disproportionate to a dataset with only a few dozen fraud examples and would have complicated the calibration story, since neural networks are well known to be poorly calibrated by default (Guo et al., 2017), which would have muddied the comparison between calibration methods with a confound about calibration across architectures.

2.3.2              Calibrating the Predicted Probabilities

Elkan’s threshold formula (Section 2.4) is only meaningful if P(fraud | x) is an accurate probability rather than merely a score that ranks transactions correctly by relative risk a model can have excellent discrimination while being badly calibrated. Every call to FraudMLPipeline.fit() therefore ts three variants unconditionally: the raw base classi er, a Platt-scaled version (Platt, 1999) ( tting a logistic sigmoid on top of the base model’s raw scores), and an isotonic-regression version ( tting a non-parametric, monotonic step function instead), both via scikit-learn’s CalibratedClassifierCV(method=..., cv=3), so the dashboard’s calibration selector is a free choice between three already-trained models rather than a re- t on demand. evaluate_calibration() computes four quantities for every variant: Brier score and log-loss (both reward probabilities close to the true 0/1 outcome), precision-recall AUC (ranking quality under class imbalance, following Davis and Goadrich, 2006), and the reliability-curve points used to draw the calibration plots in Tab 4. Accuracy is never reported anywhere in the dashboard: with a 0.172% fraud rate, a classi er that predicts genuine for every transaction achieves over 99.8% accuracy while catching zero fraud, so accuracy would actively reward the wrong behaviour here.

2.4.2             Proof of Elkan’s Threshold Theorem

τ∗ is not an arbitrary rule of thumb: Theorem 2.1 is the solution to a simple expected-cost minimisation problem, and the proof below is what justi es calling it optimal at all rather than merely conventional.

Proof. Consider a single transaction x with true fraud probability p = P(fraud | x). Predicting fraud is wrong, and incurs cost C(FP), only in the (1−p) fraction of cases where x is actually genuine; predicting genuine is wrong, and incurs cost C(FN), only in the p fraction of cases where x is actually fraud:

               E[cost | predict fraud] = (1 − p)C(FP),                      E[cost | predict genuine] = pC(FN).

2.5      The Contested Zone:       An Original Operationalisation of Vagueness

2.5.1            Motivation for the Contested Zone

Reading the Sorites literature during the proposal stage surfaced a distinction, due independently to several of the classical responses to the paradox (Hyde and Ra man, 2018; Keefe, 2000), between two di erent kinds of vagueness. First-order vagueness is simply the existence of borderline cases along a single, agreed scale: a transaction scoring P(fraud) ≈ τ for whatever τ has been xed. Higher-order vagueness is uncertainty about where the scale’s own boundary should sit in the rst place reasonable people disagree about where the line is, not merely about which side of a xed line a case falls on. Most treatments of classi cation uncertainty in the machine learning literature address only the rst kind. This project’s original contribution, the Contested Zone, operationalises the second, harder kind directly.

2.5.2               Formal De nition, as Actually Implemented

analyze_contested_zone(df_test, y_probas, c_fp_range, c_fn) takes a range of plausible C(FP) values, [cfp,min,cfp,max ], together with a single shared C(FN), converts each end of the range into a threshold via Elkan’s formula (τmin = τ∗(cfp,min,cfn), τmax = τ∗(cfp,max,cfn)), and de nes a transaction as contested exactly when τmin ≤ p(x) < τmax: its label would be fraud under the low-C(FP) (risk-averse) assumption but genuine under the high-C(FP) (frictionaverse) assumption. This is a real, useful, and honest simpli cation of the fully general twostakeholder design sketched at proposal stage, worth stating plainly rather than glossing over: the implemented version varies only the two stakeholders’ C(FP) assumption while holding C(FN) xed between them, rather than letting both stakeholders state an entirely independent (C(FP),C(FN)) pair. This is a narrower, single-parameter disagreement rather than the twodimensional disagreement the fully general version would model, and it means the zone is always an interval in τ-space by construction rather than something that needs to be shown to reduce to one. Extending the function to accept two fully independent cost pairs is noted as concrete future work in Section 5.2; as implemented, it is a correct and useful special case, not a broken general one.

analyze_contested_zone() returns both thresholds, the contested count and proportion,

the total transaction Amount sitting inside the zone, and the number of contested transactions that are actually fraud the amount total is included because a fraud-operations team would care about nancial exposure, not merely a raw transaction count, and this is precisely the quantity that turned out to be computed incorrectly in the live dashboard, as Section 2.7 explains.

The clearest, and least anticipated, nding is that Logistic Regression consistently outperformed both tree-based models on this dataset. Averaged across all three calibration variants under class weighting, Logistic Regression reaches a mean PR-AUC of 0.902, against XGBoost’s 0.875 and Random Forest’s 0.727 (full per-con guration gures in Appendix B). This runs counter to the common expectation that a tree ensemble should outperform a linear baseline, and it is worth being honest about why rather than quietly reporting only the headline numbers. The most likely explanation is sample size: with only 86 fraud transactions in the whole dataset and roughly 69 of them in the training fold, the tree ensembles’ greater capacity is prone to over tting patterns speci c to those examples that do not generalise to the 17 heldout fraud cases; Logistic Regression’s much smaller hypothesis space is, in this speci c low-data regime, an advantage rather than a limitation. A second contributing factor is that the synthetic generator (Section 2.2) creates fraud/genuine separation via a xed additive shift in ve latent features, which is close to linearly separable by construction a data-generating process that structurally favours a linear model. This is an important limitation of using synthetic data, honestly acknowledged: this result should not be read as evidence that Logistic Regression would beat XGBoost or Random Forest on the authentic ULB dataset, whose true fraud/genuine boundary is considerably more complex than the generator’s construction, only as evidence that model complexity is not automatically rewarded when the positive class is this sparse.

A second, weaker pattern across the grid is that imbalance handling did not clearly help Logistic Regression’s ranking metric: the mean PR-AUC across calibration variants was 0.926 with no imbalance handling, 0.902 with class weighting, and 0.903 with SMOTE all three within a narrow band, and, if anything, the unweighted baseline scored marginally highest. 4.1         Data Ethics

The ULB dataset (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2015) was collected and anonymised by its original authors before being made publicly available for research; this project neither collected nor could reidentify any of the underlying cardholders, since V1 V28 are the output of a one-way PCA transformation performed by the original authors, not by this project, and no key or method for reversing that transformation was ever available to this project. Where the authentic le is unavailable, the bundled generator (Section 2.2) produces data statistically resembling the real dataset’s published summary statistics but corresponding to no real person or transaction whatsoever. No transaction-level output in this dissertation, in the dashboard’s screenshots, or in its CSV exports, contains or could be traced to real personal data.

4.2             Consequences of the System’s Errors

A fraud-detection system’s two error types have genuinely asymmetric human consequences, which is precisely the asymmetry this project’s central formula is designed to make explicit rather than hide inside a single accuracy number. A false positive means a genuine customer’s transaction is blocked or agged: at best an inconvenience, at worst (if it happens at a critical moment paying for fuel, medicine, or an emergency) a serious disruption to that person’s life, and repeated false positives against the same demographic could constitute a fairness harm if the underlying model’s errors are not evenly distributed across customer groups. A false negative means a fraudulent transaction succeeds, which can cause direct nancial loss to a cardholder or merchant and can, in aggregate, raise the cost of the payment system for everyone. Elkan’s formula (Section 2.4) does not resolve this tension it cannot, because C(FP) and C(FN) are stated as external inputs, not derived from the data but it makes the tension

4.3      Accuracy of Presented Information:  the Invented Feature Labels

A narrower but genuine accuracy concern, rst raised on technical grounds in Section 2.2.3, deserves restating here as an ethical one. The dashboard labels the 28 anonymised PCA components with concrete, plausible-sounding descriptions (for example, Failed PIN / Auth Attempt Counter ), invented for this project because the dataset’s true feature semantics were never published by its original authors and are not recoverable from the released data. No transaction-level harm follows directly from this, since the underlying numbers and the model’s behaviour are una ected either way, but presenting invented labels alongside real statistical output, without a clear in-application caveat that they are illustrative rather than authoritative, risks a user walking away with unwarranted con dence about why the model agged a given transaction. This is a small-scale instance of a well-known broader concern in applied machine learning that plausible-sounding explanations are not the same as true ones and it is recorded here as a speci c, concrete lesson rather than a purely abstract principle, with a x proposed in Section 5.2.

4.4                Fairness and the Limits of This Project’s Scope

This project does not evaluate whether the classi ers’ false-positive or false-negative rates are evenly distributed across any demographic or behavioural subgroup, because the ULB dataset’s PCA-anonymised features make any such subgroup analysis impossible by design the very anonymisation that protects cardholder privacy also removes the information a fairness audit would need. This is stated here explicitly as a scope limitation rather than a claim that the system is fair: a real deployment built on this project’s ideas would need a fairness audit using a dataset (or a data-access agreement) that permits one, which the publicly released ULB dataset does not.

4.5           Dual-Use Considerations

A tool that helps a bank tune its fraud threshold could, in principle, also help a sophisticated fraudster understand roughly where a threshold sits and craft transactions just below it. This risk is judged low for this speci c project for two reasons: rst, the dashboard operates entirely on a model trained by, and threshold chosen by, its own user, so it reveals nothing about any speci c deployed bank’s actual threshold; second, the underlying idea that a cost-sensitive threshold exists and can be derived from Elkan’s formula is established, published, freely available literature from 2001, not a novel attack technique this project introduces. The Contested Zone concept, if anything, is more useful to a defender deciding where to route borderline cases for manual review than it is to an attacker.

Chapter 5

Conclusion and Future Work

5.1              Summary of This Project’s Results

This project set out to argue, and then to build working evidence for, a speci c claim: that the decision threshold in a fraud classi er is a value judgement rather than a fact waiting to be discovered, and that the philosophical vocabulary of the Sorites Paradox is a genuinely useful lens for talking about that judgement, not merely a decorative framing device. Threshold Lab operationalises this claim in three concrete ways. First, Elkan’s cost-sensitive threshold formula (Section 2.4) is implemented, tested, and exposed live, so that a user can watch the correct boundary move in real time as they change what a false positive and a false negative are worth. Second, calibration is treated as a rst-class concern rather than an afterthought (Section 2.3), since a threshold formula is only as trustworthy as the probabilities it is applied to. Third, and most originally, the Contested Zone (Section 2.5) operationalises higher-order vagueness disagreement about where the boundary itself should sit as a concrete, measurable, unit-tested quantity: on the default con guration, up to 1.06% of transactions, worth several thousand pounds in this sample alone, sit in a zone of legitimate disagreement between two reasonable cost assumptions.

The evaluation in Chapter 3 was written to be useful rather than attering. All 7 automated tests pass and six of eight stated requirements were met to a working, internally-veri ed standard (Table 3.3), but several results are reported candidly because they complicate the tidiest possible story: Logistic Regression outperformed both Random Forest and XGBoost by a clear margin on this dataset’s extremely small fraud count; neither class weighting nor SMOTE clearly outperformed the other, or even the unweighted baseline; two of the dashboard’s advertised model and imbalance options depend on packages that are not pinned in requirements.txt and silently degrade if absent; and testing the nished dashboard end-to-end, rather than only its underlying functions, surfaced a genuine row-misalignment defect in two tabs that the unit-test suite could not have caught by design (Section 2.7). None of this invalidates the project’s central argument about thresholds and calibration, but all of it is reported because reporting it honestly was judged more valuable than omitting it or hedging around it.

5.2.1        Immediate               xes identi ed by this dissertation’s own testing

Three concrete, well-understood xes were identi ed while writing this dissertation and are listed here, in priority order, ahead of the larger pieces of future work below, precisely because none of them requires new design thinking, only implementation time that was not available before submission. 5.2.2              A human evaluation of the Contested Zone

The single most valuable next step, agged honestly as a gap in Section 3.6.1, is a small structured study in which real people ideally drawn from, or with direct experience adjacent to, fraudoperations and customer-experience roles are asked to state their own (C(FP),C(FN)) cost assumptions independently, rather than using this project’s two illustrative stakeholder personas. A recruitment target of 4 6 participants per role, an information sheet explaining the (entirely synthetic or already-anonymised) data involved, and a short consent form covering voluntary participation and the right to withdraw would be the minimum needed to run this ethically; none of this was collected for the present project (Section 3.6.1), so no such consent form is included in the appendices, but a template for one, adapted from the module’s own ethical guidance, would be the rst artefact produced before recruiting anyone. The resulting spread of real stated cost ratios rather than this project’s own two illustrative points could then be fed directly into contested_zone_sweep() with no code changes required, since the function already accepts an arbitrary threshold grid.

5.2.3              Validation on the authentic ULB dataset

Every quantitative result in Chapter 3 uses the bundled synthetic generator, because the authentic 143MB creditcard.csv le is not distributed with the assessment materials. The dashboard already supports uploading or auto-detecting that le with no code changes (Section 2.2), so the immediate next step, achievable in well under a day, is simply to re-run experiments.py against the authentic data and compare the resulting model-comparison grid (Section 3.4) against the synthetic-data grid reported here, to establish how much of the Logistic-Regression-beatsXGBoost nding is an artefact of the synthetic generator’s near-linear separability versus a genuine property of the underlying fraud-detection task.

5.2.4         Fairness auditing

As discussed in Chapter 4, the ULB dataset’s PCA-anonymised features make a demographic fairness audit impossible on this speci c data. A natural extension would use a dataset, or a dataaccess agreement, that retains enough non-sensitive context (for example, merchant category or transaction channel, without any directly identifying cardholder attribute) to check whether the Contested Zone, and the classi ers’ error rates more broadly, are evenly distributed across those categories, or instead concentrate unfairly on some.

5.2.5                Extending the Contested Zone beyond two stakeholders

The current Contested Zone de nition is deliberately the simplest version that could demonstrate the concept: exactly two stakeholders, each stating a single point estimate for their cost ratio. A richer version could accept a full distribution over plausible cost ratios (re ecting genuine uncertainty about, rather than mere disagreement between, stakeholders) and report a continuous degree of contestedness per transaction the probability, under that distribution, that a randomly sampled plausible cost ratio would ip the transaction’s label which would also connect the project even more directly to the degree-theoretic (fuzzy logic) response to the Sorites Paradox discussed in the Sorites Panel (Section 2.6).

5.2.6            A human-in-the-loop review queue

The most directly deployable extension would turn the Contested Zone from an analytical report into an operational work ow: rather than a single automated label, transactions falling inside the zone would be routed to a human reviewer, while transactions clearly outside it in either direction remain fully automated. This is essentially the nihilism about the predicate response surveyed in the Sorites Panel (Section 2.6) turned into a concrete system design: rather than forcing every transaction through a single automated cuto , the boundary cases are routed to a process better suited to handling genuine indeterminacy than a hard threshold ever can be.

5.3                 Closing Remark: Making the Arbitrary Visible

The Sorites Paradox has no universally agreed resolution after roughly 2,400 years of philosophical attention (Hyde and Ra man, 2018), and this project does not claim to have resolved it for fraud detection either. What it does claim, and what the evidence in Chapter 3 supports, is narrower and more useful: that treating a classi cation threshold as an explicit, cost-derived, contestable value judgement rather than a silent default of 0.5 surfaces real information (the 47.2% cost reduction in Section 3.3, the 0.10% 1.06% of genuinely disputed transactions in Section 3.5) that a conventional single-threshold dashboard would leave invisible. Somewhere between a heap of ten thousand grains and none at all, there is no non-arbitrary place to draw the line; the practical response this project o ers is not to nd that place, but to make its arbitrariness, and its cost, visible.

This is not to say the wider literature is short of proposed resolutions only that none commands universal agreement. One instructive comparison comes from outside computer science and outside fraud detection entirely: crystallography faces its own version of the sorites problem when deciding whether two measured crystal structures are the same or di erent , a judgement traditionally made as a binary yes/no despite resting on continuous measurements that are never exactly equal in practice. Anosova et al. (2024) argue that this binary framing is itself the source of the di culty, and propose resolving it not by locating the boundary more precisely but by replacing it altogether with a continuous distance metric between crystal structures, so that are these the same? is replaced by the strictly more informative how far apart are these? . Credit-card fraud detection cannot adopt that resolution directly: a transaction is, operationally, either approved or blocked, so unlike a crystal-structure comparison, this project’s continuous risk score p(x) must eventually be collapsed into a binary action at the point of decision, however carefully that collapse is justi ed (Section 2.4). What Threshold Lab’s continuous risk score and Contested Zone do share with the crystallographic approach, despite that di erence, is the underlying idea that most of the philosophical work is better done before the binary collapse than after it: by making the continuous quantity and the boundary’s own uncertainty explicit and inspectable, rather than reporting only the nal binary label. Readers interested in a fuller philosophical treatment of the Sorites Paradox than the brief survey in Section 2.6 allows are directed to Oms and Zardini (2019), a comprehensive edited collection devoted entirely to the paradox and its proposed solutions.

import numpy as np import pandas as pd

def compute_elkan_threshold(c_fp: float, c_fn: float) -> float:

"""

Computes the optimal expected-cost-minimizing threshold tau* according to Elkan (2001):

tau* = C(FP) / (C(FP) + C(FN))

"""

if c_fp <= 0 or c_fn <= 0:

raise ValueError("Costs must be strictly positive numbers.")

return c_fp / (c_fp + c_fn)
